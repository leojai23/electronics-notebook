Before you start

Four safety rules for every build

  • Check first. Your guide looks over the wiring before power goes on.
  • Unplug to change. Disconnect all power before moving any wire.
  • Hot, smelly or smoking? Unplug right away. Tell your guide and let it cool.
  • Low power only. Use USB and AA batteries. Never connect anything here to a wall socket.

Breadboard

Unit I · Topic 1

1. Overview

A breadboard lets you build a real circuit with no soldering. Push parts in, pull them out, and try again. Think of it as LEGO for electronics. Every build in this notebook starts here.

2. Detailed Notes

2.1 Name and look

A white plastic slab covered in small holes, with red and blue lines along its long edges. It has no circuit symbol, because it only holds the parts.

2.2 Purpose

It connects parts for you. Metal strips hidden under the holes join them in lines, so two legs in the same line are connected.

2.3 Key properties

PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
Size400 or 830 holesBigger boards fit bigger circuits.
Hole spacing2.54 mmFits most beginner parts.
Joined groups5 holes eachEach group sits on its own metal strip.
Power rails2 lines per sideCarry + and − along the whole board.

2.4 Example everyday usage

You won't find one inside a phone or a remote. Engineers use them to test ideas before they pay for a permanent circuit board.

2.5 Sample robotics application

It's the meeting point of your robot. The sensor, resistors and transistor all plug in here and connect back to the Arduino.

2.6 Handling and safety

  • Unplug the power before moving any wire.
  • Use solid jumper wires, and push legs straight in so they don't bend.
  • Treat it as a low-power tool. If a wire feels warm, unplug and look for a short circuit.
  • On some long boards the power rails break in the middle. Look for a gap in the printed line.

3. Key Terms

Jumper wire
A short wire with solid ends, used to link holes on a breadboard.
Power rail
A long joined line of holes that carries + or − along the board.
Prototype
A test version of a circuit, built to check that an idea works.
Short circuit
An accidental path with almost no resistance. It makes things hot fast.

Exam-Prep Summary

  • A breadboard needs no soldering, so parts can be moved freely.
  • Holes in a group of 5 are joined. The centre gap separates the two halves.
  • Power rails run the length of the board, and + and − stay separate.
  • Always unplug the power before changing wires.

4. Practice Questions

  1. Why do engineers use breadboards before making a circuit board? (Short answer)
    Show model answer
    Parts can be moved and changed in seconds, with no soldering. That makes it cheap and quick to test an idea and fix mistakes.
  2. Two LED legs are pushed into the same group of 5 holes. What happens, and why? (Applied)
    Show model answer
    The LED won't light. Both legs share one metal strip, so they're joined together and current goes around the LED through the strip.
  3. Your circuit works at one end of a long breadboard but not at the other. What would you check first? (Applied)
    Show model answer
    Check whether the power rail has a break in the middle. If it does, add a jumper wire across the gap.

    Resistor

    Unit I · Topic 2

    1. Overview

    A resistor limits how much current can flow. In the water model, it's the narrow bit of pipe. You'll use one nearly every time you connect an LED or a transistor to the Arduino, because it stops too much current rushing through.

    2. Detailed Notes

    2.1 Name and look

    A tiny cylinder with coloured stripes and two wire legs. Its circuit symbol is a zigzag line or a plain rectangle.

    2.2 Purpose

    It controls current. Bigger resistance means less flow, so a resistor protects parts that would be damaged by too much current.

    2.3 Key properties

    PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
    ResistanceOhms (Ω)1 kΩ means 1,000 Ω.
    Colour bands220 Ω is red-red-brown1 kΩ is brown-black-red. 10 kΩ is brown-black-orange.
    ToleranceGold band: ±5%How close it is to its label.
    Power ratingUsually ¼ wattHow much heat it can safely handle.

    2.4 Example everyday usage

    Almost every circuit board has them. In a TV remote, one sets how hard the signal light is driven.

    2.5 Sample robotics application

    A resistor protects each LED on your robot. Another protects the Arduino pin that controls a transistor.

    2.6 Handling and safety

    • It works either way round, so you can't plug it in backwards.
    • Check the value before using it. The stripes are small, so a phone camera or a meter helps.
    • A hot resistor means too much current. Unplug it and recheck your value.

    3. Key Terms

    Ohm (Ω)
    The unit of resistance.
    Ohm's Law
    Voltage = current × resistance.
    Tolerance
    How far the real value may be from the printed value.
    Current limiting
    Using a resistor to keep current at a safe level.

    Exam-Prep Summary

    • A resistor limits current. It has no + or − side.
    • Current = voltage ÷ resistance.
    • Colour bands give the value: digit, digit, multiplier, tolerance.
    • A hot resistor is a warning sign, so unplug and recheck.

    4. Practice Questions

    1. Read the bands: brown, black, red, gold. (Short answer)
      Show model answer
      1, 0, then ×100, which gives 1,000 Ω or 1 kΩ. Gold means within 5%.
    2. An LED needs 2 V from a 5 V pin, with a 220 Ω resistor. Roughly how much current flows? (Applied)
      Show model answer
      The resistor has 5 V − 2 V = 3 V across it. 3 V ÷ 220 Ω is about 0.014 A, or 14 mA.
    3. Why might you swap a 220 Ω resistor for a 1 kΩ one? (Applied)
      Show model answer
      To lower the current. It makes an LED dimmer, and it keeps an Uno R4 under its 8 mA pin limit.

      LED (Light Emitting Diode)

      Unit I · Topic 3

      1. Overview

      An LED turns electricity into light. It's also a one-way valve, so current only flows through it in one direction. It's the quickest way to see that your code is running, which makes it the perfect first output.

      2. Detailed Notes

      2.1 Name and look

      A small coloured plastic dome with two legs. Its symbol is a triangle pointing at a bar, with two small arrows for the light.

      2.2 Purpose

      It gives light from a small amount of current. On a robot, that means status lights you can read at a glance.

      2.3 Key properties

      PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
      Forward voltageAbout 2 V red, 3 V blue or whiteThe push it needs to light up.
      Forward currentUp to about 20 mAMuch less still glows nicely.
      PolarityLong leg +, short leg −The flat edge is on the − side.

      2.4 Example everyday usage

      Phone flashlights, TV standby lights, traffic lights, and the infrared light on the front of a remote control.

      2.5 Sample robotics application

      Status lights. Red can mean "wall ahead" and green can mean "path clear". It's also how you test that a pin is working.

      2.6 Handling and safety

      • Never connect an LED without a resistor. Too much current will kill it, sometimes with a small pop.
      • Backwards usually won't hurt it, but it won't light. Flip it and try again.
      • Don't stare into a very bright LED.
      • On an Uno R3, a 220 Ω resistor gives about 14 mA. On an Uno R4, use 1 kΩ, which gives about 3 mA and stays under its 8 mA limit.

      3. Key Terms

      Anode
      The + leg of an LED, usually the longer one.
      Cathode
      The − leg of an LED, usually the shorter one.
      Diode
      A part that lets current flow in one direction only.
      Forward voltage
      The voltage an LED needs before it conducts and lights.

      Exam-Prep Summary

      • An LED only conducts in one direction: long leg +, short leg −.
      • Red needs about 2 V, and blue or white needs about 3 V.
      • Always use a series resistor to limit the current.
      • If it doesn't light, check its direction first.

      4. Practice Questions

      1. Your LED doesn't light, but your code is correct. Name two things to check. (Short answer)
        Show model answer
        Check that it's the right way round, with the long leg towards the pin. Then check that its legs are in different groups on the breadboard.
      2. Why can't you connect an LED straight to a 5 V pin? (Short answer)
        Show model answer
        Nothing would limit the current. Too much would flow and damage the LED, and possibly the pin.
      3. A red and a blue LED each use a 220 Ω resistor on 5 V. Which gets more current? (Applied)
        Show model answer
        The red one. It uses about 2 V, leaving 3 V across the resistor. The blue uses about 3 V, leaving only 2 V.

        Capacitor

        Unit I · Topic 4

        1. Overview

        A capacitor stores a small amount of charge and releases it fast. Picture a little water tank on the pipe. When demand suddenly jumps, the tank covers the gap so the pressure doesn't drop. Robots need this, because motors grab power in sudden gulps.

        2. Detailed Notes

        2.1 Name and look

        There are two common types: a tiny ceramic disc, and a small metal can called an electrolytic. Its symbol is two short parallel lines, with a + mark on polarised types.

        2.2 Purpose

        It smooths the power supply. It charges up when power is plentiful, and gives it back when something suddenly needs more.

        2.3 Key properties

        PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
        CapacitanceFarads (F)Real parts use µF, nF and pF.
        Voltage ratingPrinted on the partChoose well above your supply. For 6 V, use 16 V or more.
        PolarityElectrolytic onlyThe stripe and short leg mark the − side. Small ceramic ones work either way.

        2.4 Example everyday usage

        Phones and chargers use them to keep power smooth. A camera flash uses a big one to dump its energy in a burst of light.

        2.5 Sample robotics application

        When a servo starts moving, it grabs a gulp of current. A 470 µF capacitor across its power lines helps keep the supply steady, so your circuit doesn't glitch.

        2.6 Handling and safety

        • Connect electrolytic types the right way round, and stay under the voltage rating. Get either wrong and they can overheat, swell or burst.
        • Never open a phone charger, camera flash, microwave or other mains device. Large capacitors inside can hold a dangerous charge even when unplugged.
        • Small capacitors in these builds are low risk, but unplug the circuit before touching them.
        • If one bulges or feels warm, unplug it and replace it.

        3. Key Terms

        Farad (F)
        The unit of capacitance.
        Microfarad (µF)
        One millionth of a farad.
        Polarised
        Having a + and a − side that must be connected correctly.
        Decoupling
        Using a capacitor to smooth out quick dips in the power supply.

        Exam-Prep Summary

        • A capacitor stores charge and releases it quickly.
        • Value is measured in farads, usually µF, nF or pF.
        • On an electrolytic, the stripe marks the − side.
        • Choose a voltage rating well above your supply.

        4. Practice Questions

        1. What does the printed code 104 mean on a ceramic capacitor? (Short answer)
          Show model answer
          10 followed by 4 zeros, in picofarads. That's 100,000 pF, or 100 nF.
        2. Your Arduino resets each time the servo starts. How can a capacitor help, and what else should you change? (Applied)
          Show model answer
          A capacitor across the servo's power lines covers the sudden demand. You should also power the servo from its own batteries, joined to Arduino GND.
        3. A 6 V robot has a choice of 6.3 V or 16 V capacitors. Which do you choose, and why? (Applied)
          Show model answer
          The 16 V one. Fresh batteries can read above 6 V, so 6.3 V leaves almost no safety margin.

          Transistor

          Unit I · Topic 5

          1. Overview

          A transistor is an electronic switch. A tiny current controls a much bigger one. Think of a big water valve that turns with a light touch of your finger. It's how a weak Arduino pin gets to control a hungry motor.

          2. Detailed Notes

          2.1 Name and look

          A small black part with three legs. It comes in a half-round shape or a square body with a metal tab. Its symbol is a line with three branches, and one has an arrow.

          2.2 Purpose

          It lets a small signal switch a large current. The Arduino only sends the signal, and the battery supplies the power.

          2.3 Key properties

          PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
          TypeNPN or PNPThis notebook uses NPN.
          LegsBase, collector, emitterBase is the control. Current goes in the collector and out the emitter.
          Maximum ratingsTIP120: 5 A, 60 VThe most it can safely switch.
          GainWritten hFEHow much bigger the controlled current can be.

          2.4 Example everyday usage

          Everywhere. The chip in a phone contains billions of microscopic transistors, all switching on and off to do the computing.

          2.5 Sample robotics application

          An Arduino pin is too weak to run a motor. So the pin signals a transistor, and the transistor switches the motor's battery power on and off.

          2.6 Handling and safety

          • Check the leg order for your exact part number. It varies, even between parts that look identical.
          • TIP120 legs, with the printed side facing you and legs down: base, collector, emitter, from left to right.
          • Always put a resistor on the base. A TIP120 driving a motor uses 1 kΩ.
          • The TIP120's metal tab is joined to its collector. Keep it from touching other parts or wires.
          • Getting warm is normal. To check, unplug the power first, then touch it carefully. If it's too hot to hold a finger on, look for a stalled motor or a wiring error.

          3. Key Terms

          Base
          The control leg of a transistor.
          Collector
          The leg where the large current enters an NPN transistor.
          Emitter
          The leg where the current leaves an NPN transistor.
          NPN
          A transistor type that switches on when its base is driven high.
          Gain (hFE)
          How much a transistor multiplies the base current.

          Exam-Prep Summary

          • A transistor uses a small base current to control a large current.
          • The three legs are base, collector and emitter.
          • Leg order depends on the part, so always check.
          • A base resistor protects both the pin and the transistor.

          4. Practice Questions

          1. Why can't you drive a motor straight from an Arduino pin? (Short answer)
            Show model answer
            A motor needs far more current than a pin can safely supply. A transistor handles the large current, using only a small signal from the pin.
          2. About how much base current flows through a 1 kΩ resistor from a 5 V pin into a TIP120? (Applied)
            Show model answer
            The TIP120's base uses about 1.4 V, so 3.6 V is left across the resistor. 3.6 V ÷ 1,000 Ω is about 3.6 mA.
          3. The motor runs all the time, even when the code says off. What might be wrong? (Applied)
            Show model answer
            The collector and emitter may be swapped or shorted, or the motor may bypass the transistor. Unplug and check each leg against the data sheet.

            DC Motor

            Unit I · Topic 6

            1. Overview

            A DC motor turns electricity into spinning. It's the water wheel in the pipe model: flow pushes it, and it turns. Geared DC motors drive the wheels of most starter robots.

            2. Detailed Notes

            2.1 Name and look

            A small metal cylinder with a spinning shaft and two wires. Robot versions often come in a yellow gearbox. Its symbol is a circle with an M inside.

            2.2 Purpose

            It creates movement. Connect power and it spins until you cut the power.

            2.3 Key properties

            PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
            VoltageOften 3–6 VWhat it's designed to run on.
            CurrentAmps (A)Stall current is the big one: the draw when the shaft is stuck.
            SpeedRPMTurns per minute.
            TorqueOften kg·cmTurning strength. Gears trade speed for more torque.

            2.4 Example everyday usage

            Toy cars, computer fans, electric toothbrushes and cordless drills.

            2.5 Sample robotics application

            Geared DC motors drive your robot's wheels. Swap the two wires and it spins the other way. Pulse the power quickly and it runs slower.

            2.6 Handling and safety

            • Never connect a motor directly to an Arduino pin. That can destroy the pin.
            • Use a separate battery and a transistor, and add a protective diode across the motor. When a motor switches off, it can kick back a voltage spike.
            • Keep fingers, hair and loose sleeves away from anything spinning.
            • A stuck motor gets hot quickly. If it stalls, buzzes or smells, unplug it.
            • One transistor runs one motor in one direction. A two-wheeled robot normally uses a motor driver board.

            3. Key Terms

            RPM
            Revolutions per minute, a measure of speed.
            Torque
            Turning strength.
            Stall current
            The large current a motor draws when its shaft can't turn.
            PWM
            Pulse-width modulation: switching power on and off quickly to control speed.
            Flyback diode
            A diode across a motor that safely absorbs its switch-off spike.

            Exam-Prep Summary

            • A DC motor spins when connected, and swapping its wires reverses it.
            • Stall current is much higher than running current.
            • Drive motors through a transistor or a driver board, from a separate supply.
            • A flyback diode protects the circuit from switch-off spikes.

            4. Practice Questions

            1. How can you make a DC motor spin the other way? (Short answer)
              Show model answer
              Swap its two wires, so that the current flows through it in the opposite direction.
            2. Why does a stalled motor get hot? (Applied)
              Show model answer
              With the shaft stuck, it draws its largest current and all that power becomes heat. It's doing no useful work.
            3. Why add a diode across a motor that's switched by a transistor? (Applied)
              Show model answer
              When the motor switches off, it produces a voltage spike. The diode gives that spike a safe path, which protects the transistor.

              Servo Motor

              Unit I · Topic 7

              1. Overview

              A servo motor moves to an exact angle and holds there. A DC motor is told "go". A servo is told "go to 90 degrees". That precision makes it ideal for steering, grippers and a robot's turning head.

              2. Detailed Notes

              2.1 Name and look

              A small plastic box with a shaft, a plastic arm called a horn, and three wires. Circuit diagrams often show a labelled box.

              2.2 Purpose

              It gives controlled positioning. Inside are a small motor, gears, a position sensor and a control circuit.

              2.3 Key properties

              PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
              AngleUsually 0–180°Its range of movement.
              VoltageSG90: about 4.8–6 VCheck your own servo's label.
              TorqueSG90: about 1.2–1.8 kg·cmVaries by seller, so treat it as a guide.
              SignalShort pulsesThe length of each pulse sets the angle.
              Wires (SG90)Brown, red, orangeGround, power, signal. Clones can differ.

              2.4 Example everyday usage

              Steering on a remote-control car, moving flaps on a model plane, and the joints of a robot arm.

              2.5 Sample robotics application

              Mount the distance sensor on a servo. Now your robot can turn its head and look left and right before choosing a way to go.

              2.6 Handling and safety

              • Don't force the arm by hand. The small plastic gears inside can strip.
              • Give it its own battery power. Its sudden current gulps can reset the Arduino, and Arduino's Uno R4 manual also says to use an external supply for servos.
              • Join the battery's negative to the Arduino's GND. Without a shared ground, the servo may jitter or ignore you.
              • Buzzing and getting hot usually means it's straining against something. Unplug it and free the arm.
              • On an Uno R3, the Servo library turns off speed control (analogWrite) on pins 9 and 10.

              3. Key Terms

              Horn
              The plastic arm attached to a servo's shaft.
              Shared ground
              Joining the − sides of two supplies so that signals have a common reference.
              Jitter
              Small unwanted shaking, often caused by noisy power or a missing ground.
              Pulse
              A brief on-signal. Its length tells a servo where to go.

              Exam-Prep Summary

              • A servo goes to a set angle and holds it, usually between 0 and 180°.
              • Its three wires are ground, power and signal.
              • Power it from batteries, with the grounds joined.
              • Don't force the horn by hand, because the gears can strip.

              4. Practice Questions

              1. When would you pick a servo over a DC motor? (Short answer)
                Show model answer
                When you need an exact position, such as steering or turning a sensor. A DC motor is better for continuous spinning, such as driving wheels.
              2. Your servo jitters and ignores commands, but it has fresh batteries. What's the likely cause? (Applied)
                Show model answer
                A missing shared ground. Join the battery's − to Arduino GND so that the signal has a common reference.
              3. Why does a straining servo get hot? (Applied)
                Show model answer
                It keeps pushing against the blockage and draws high current, and that power turns into heat.

                Ultrasonic Distance Sensor

                Unit I · Topic 8

                1. Overview

                An ultrasonic sensor measures distance using sound. Shout in a canyon and time the echo, and you can work out how far away the wall is. Bats do the same thing. This sensor gives your robot its sense of what's ahead.

                2. Detailed Notes

                2.1 Name and look

                A small board with two silver cylinders that look like eyes, and four pins: VCC, Trig, Echo and GND. It has no standard symbol, so diagrams show a labelled box.

                2.2 Purpose

                It tells your robot how far away things are. One cylinder sends a pulse of sound, and the other listens for the echo.

                2.3 Key properties

                PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
                RangeAbout 2–400 cmClosest and farthest it can measure.
                Sound frequency40 kHzToo high for you to hear.
                Beam widthAbout 15°It sees a narrow cone straight ahead.
                Power5 V, about 15 mAFine to run from the Uno's 5V pin.
                TimingWait over 60 msLeave this gap between readings so that echoes don't mix.

                2.4 Example everyday usage

                Car parking sensors use the same echo idea to beep as you near a wall.

                2.5 Sample robotics application

                It's your robot's eyes. In a loop, the Arduino asks "how far?" and stops the motors if the answer is under 20 cm.

                2.6 Handling and safety

                • Double-check VCC and GND. Swapping them can destroy the sensor.
                • Don't trust it blindly. Pillows and curtains soak up sound, and walls at a steep angle bounce it away.
                • It's a 5 V part, which suits the Uno. A 3.3 V board such as a Raspberry Pi Pico needs extra parts to read the Echo pin safely.
                • If it gets warm, unplug straight away and check the wiring.

                3. Key Terms

                Ultrasonic
                Sound above the range of human hearing.
                Trig
                The pin that tells the sensor to send a pulse.
                Echo
                The pin that reports how long the sound took to return.
                Microsecond (µs)
                One millionth of a second.

                Exam-Prep Summary

                • The sensor times an echo to measure distance.
                • Distance = echo time × speed of sound ÷ 2.
                • Range is about 2–400 cm, with a narrow beam of about 15°.
                • Soft and angled surfaces can fool it.

                  Microcontroller (Arduino Uno)

                  Unit I · Topic 9

                  1. Overview

                  A microcontroller is a tiny computer on one chip. The Arduino Uno is a board built around one, with a USB socket and rows of pin sockets. It's the brain: it reads the sensors, runs your code, and tells everything else what to do.

                  2. Detailed Notes

                  2.1 Name and look

                  A small circuit board with a USB socket, a power jack, a main chip and two rows of pin sockets. It usually appears as a labelled box in diagrams.

                  2.2 Purpose

                  It runs the sense-think-act loop, over and over, many times a second.

                  2.3 Key properties

                  PropertyUnit or valueWhat it means
                  Chip (Uno R3)ATmega328P, 16 MHz16 million ticks each second.
                  Memory32 kB flash, 2 kB SRAMTiny next to a phone, and plenty for this notebook.
                  Pins14 digital, 6 analog input6 of the digital pins can do PWM.
                  Voltage5 VUSB supplies the power.
                  Pin currentStay under 20 mA40 mA is the damage limit. The Uno R4 allows only 8 mA.

                  2.4 Example everyday usage

                  Microwave ovens, washing machines, TV remotes and toys. Most gadgets with buttons have one hidden inside.

                  2.5 Sample robotics application

                  It reads the distance, decides what to do, then controls the motors and LEDs.

                  2.6 Handling and safety

                  • Never connect the 5V pin directly to GND. That's a short circuit.
                  • Pins signal, they don't power. Motors and servos need their own supply.
                  • Don't connect anything over 5 V to a pin.
                  • Keep the board away from metal tools and coins, which can bridge pins underneath. Hold it by the edges.
                  • If it gets hot or won't show up on the computer, unplug it and check for shorts.
                  • Leave pins 0 and 1 empty for now, because the Arduino uses them to talk over USB.

                  Digital pin

                  Analog input
                  A pin that can read a range of voltages.
                  Flash memory
                  Where your program stays, even with the power off.
                  Sketch

                  • A microcontroller reads inputs, runs code and controls outputs.
                  • Uno R3: 14 digital pins, 6 analog inputs, 5 V, 16 MHz.
                  • Keep pin current under 20 mA on an R3, and 8 mA on an R4.
                  • Pins carry signals. Power hungry parts from a separate supply.

                    Detailed description

                    What are electronics and robotics?

                    Electronics is about controlling electricity to do useful jobs. A circuit might light a lamp, measure a temperature or run a whole phone. Robotics combines electronics, mechanics and code to make machines that sense what's around them, decide what to do, and then act.

                    These ideas are all around you. An automatic door, a washing machine and a car's parking sensor all use parts you'll meet in this notebook.

                    The big idea: electricity is like water in pipes

                    You can't see electricity, but you can picture water flowing around a closed loop of pipes. A pump pushes the water, and it comes back round to the pump.

                    WordWhat it meansWater versionMeasured in
                    VoltageThe pushPressure from the pumpVolts (V)
                    CurrentHow much is flowingWater flowing past each secondAmps (A), milliamps (mA)
                    ResistanceHow hard it is to flowA narrow bit of pipeOhms (Ω)

                    They're linked by Ohm's Law: voltage = current × resistance. More push means more flow, and a narrower pipe means less flow.

                    • Circuit: the complete loop. Break it anywhere and everything stops.
                    • Short circuit: a path with almost no resistance, like a burst pipe. Huge current rushes through and things get hot fast. This is the main mistake to watch for.

                    The analogy has limits, because real wires don't leak and water doesn't need a return pipe to flow. Treat it as a thinking tool.

                    How a robot works: sense, think, act

                    Almost every robot repeats the same three jobs. Once you can see this loop, big projects become much easier to plan.

                    Analog and digital signals

                    Parts talk to the Arduino in two basic ways. A digital signal has only two states, HIGH or LOW, like a light switch. An analog signal can be any level in a range, like a dimmer. The Sensors section also explains pulses and data signals.

                    How you tell it what to do: code

                    The Arduino runs a short program called a sketch. Every sketch has two main parts: setup() runs once at the start, and loop() runs over and over. This one blinks an LED, and you can run it in the Practical Lab.

                    const int LED_PIN = 8;   // give the pin a name

void setup() {           // runs once, at power-on
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {            // runs over and over, forever
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);
  delay(1000);           // wait 1 second
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);
  delay(1000);
}

                    Fix It Guide
                    What to do when something doesn't work
                    

                    Every builder runs into faults, and finding them is a big part of engineering. Start with the steps below, then find your problem in the list. Have your guide check anything that gets hot or smells.
                    


                    The method
                    1. Stop and unplug. If anything is hot or smells, let it cool first.
                    2. Say what you expected, and what happened. "The LED should blink, but it stays dark" is already half the answer.
                    3. Check power first. Is the Arduino's power light on? Is the battery fresh? Are the power rails connected?
                    4. Follow the wires. Trace each connection from start to end, using your finger and the steps for that build.
                    5. Check direction. LEDs, electrolytic capacitors, diodes, transistors and modules must go the right way round.
                    6. Test one thing at a time. Run a tiny sketch that tests only the part in doubt. Change one thing, then test again.
                    7. Compare with the simulator. Open the same example and check that your wiring and code match.
                    8. Write it down. Record the problem and the fix in the Build Log.
                    

                    Using a multimeter
                    

                    A multimeter is a low-cost tool that shows what's really happening. Ask your guide to help the first few times, and use it on low-voltage projects only.
                    

                    • Voltage (V): with power on, touch the probes across two points. Is there 5 V between the 5V rail and GND?
                    • Continuity (the beep setting): with power off, check whether two points are joined. Use it to find broken wires.
                    • Resistance (Ω): with power off, and the part out of the circuit, check a resistor's value.
                    

                    Uploading code
                    The upload fails or the port isn't listed
                    • The wrong board or port is selected in the Arduino software.
                    • Some USB cables only charge, and can't carry data.
                    • A wire on pin 0 or 1 is interfering with USB. Unplug it and try again.
                    • Some clone boards need a USB driver, such as CH340.
                    The code won't compile
                    • Read the first error, and look at that line and the one above it.
                    • Look for a missing semicolon or bracket.
                    • Capital letters matter: digitalWrite, not digitalwrite.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The code uploads, but nothing happens
                    • Check that the pin number in the code matches your wire.
                    • Check for pinMode(pin, OUTPUT) in setup().
                    • Try the Blink example to prove the board works.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    LEDs and resistors
                    The LED stays dark
                    • It may be backwards. The long leg goes towards the positive side.
                    • Both legs may be in the same joined group of holes.
                    • The resistor may be in the wrong row, or be the wrong value.
                    • The LED may already be burned out. Try another.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The LED is very dim
                    • The resistor value may be too high. Read its bands again.
                    • The pin may be set as an INPUT. Check pinMode.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The LED flashed once and died
                    • It had too little resistance in series, or none. Replace it and add a resistor.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    Buttons and dials
                    The button acts randomly
                    • The pin may be floating. Use INPUT_PULLUP, or a resistor to GND or 5V.
                    • A pressed button reads LOW when using INPUT_PULLUP.
                    • Real buttons bounce. A short delay helps.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The button seems always pressed
                    • It may be turned 90 degrees, so its legs are always joined. Rotate it, and place it across the centre gap.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The dial's readings don't change
                    • Check that the middle leg goes to the analog pin, and the outer legs to 5V and GND.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    Motors and servos
                    The motor doesn't turn
                    • Is the battery connected and fresh?
                    • Does the battery − join Arduino GND?
                    • Check the transistor's leg order against its data sheet.
                    • The speed may be too low. Try full speed first.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The Arduino resets when the motor or servo starts
                    • It's probably sharing power with the Arduino. Use a separate battery with a shared ground.
                    • Add a 470 µF capacitor across the motor or servo supply.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The servo jitters or ignores commands
                    • The battery − may not be joined to Arduino GND.
                    • The batteries may be weak.
                    • Check that the orange wire goes to the pin named in attach().
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    The transistor or motor gets very hot
                    • Unplug it. The motor may be stalled, or the diode may be backwards.
                    • Check the diode: its stripe faces the battery +.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    Sensors
                    The distance sensor reads 0 or never changes
                    • Check that VCC and GND are correct.
                    • Check that TRIG and ECHO aren't swapped.
                    • Soft or angled surfaces can give no echo. Test with a book.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    An analog reading jumps around
                    • Check for loose wires, and for a shared GND.
                    • Take several readings and average them.
                    • An unconnected analog pin wanders. Check the signal wire.
                    See it in the Practical Lab
                    An I2C sensor isn't found
                    • SDA goes to A4, and SCL to A5, on an Uno. Check that they aren't swapped.
                    • Check whether the board accepts 5 V. Some are 3.3 V only.
                    A library sensor gives no reading or an error
                    • Install the library named on the sensor's page.
                    • Check that the pin number in the code matches your wiring.
                    • Some sensors, such as the DHT11, can't be read too often.
                    Robots
                    The robot spins when it should go straight
                    • One motor's wires are probably reversed. Take out the batteries, and swap that motor's wires.
                    Try it in the Robot Simulator
                    The robot curves
                    • This is normal, because motors are never identical. Lower the faster motor's speed in your code.
                    Try it in the Robot Simulator
                    The robot is slow or stops working
                    • Replace or recharge the batteries first.
                    • Check that nothing is rubbing against the wheels.
                    The robot crashes into some things
                    • The sensor may miss soft objects, or things beside its beam.
                    • Make the stopping distance bigger, or slow down.
                    • Try a turning sensor head.
                    Try it in the Robot Simulator
                    The robot loses the line
                    • Slow down, and make the turns sharper.
                    • Check your sensor height, and which reading means black.
                    Try it in the Robot Simulator

                    • A capacitor has bulged, or a battery is swollen or leaking: don't use it. Ask your guide to dispose of it safely.
                    • Never test a fault by connecting anything to a wall socket.

                    Key points by topic
                    1. Breadboard
                    • A breadboard needs no soldering, so parts can be moved freely.
                    • Holes in a group of 5 are joined. The centre gap separates the two halves.
                    • Power rails run the length of the board, and + and − stay separate.
                    • Always unplug the power before changing wires.
                    2. Resistor
                    • A resistor limits current. It has no + or − side.
                    • Current = voltage ÷ resistance.
                    • Colour bands give the value: digit, digit, multiplier, tolerance.
                    • A hot resistor is a warning sign, so unplug and recheck.
                    3. LED (Light Emitting Diode)
                    • An LED only conducts in one direction: long leg +, short leg −.
                    • Red needs about 2 V, and blue or white needs about 3 V.
                    • Always use a series resistor to limit the current.
                    • If it doesn't light, check its direction first.
                    4. Capacitor
                    • A capacitor stores charge and releases it quickly.
                    • Value is measured in farads, usually µF, nF or pF.
                    • On an electrolytic, the stripe marks the − side.
                    • Choose a voltage rating well above your supply.
                    5. Transistor
                    • A transistor uses a small base current to control a large current.
                    • The three legs are base, collector and emitter.
                    • Leg order depends on the part, so always check.
                    • A base resistor protects both the pin and the transistor.
                    6. DC Motor
                    • A DC motor spins when connected, and swapping its wires reverses it.
                    • Stall current is much higher than running current.
                    • Drive motors through a transistor or a driver board, from a separate supply.
                    • A flyback diode protects the circuit from switch-off spikes.
                    7. Servo Motor
                    • A servo goes to a set angle and holds it, usually between 0 and 180°.
                    • Its three wires are ground, power and signal.
                    • Power it from batteries, with the grounds joined.
                    • Don't force the horn by hand, because the gears can strip.
                    8. Ultrasonic Distance Sensor
                    • The sensor times an echo to measure distance.
                    • Distance = echo time × speed of sound ÷ 2.
                    • Range is about 2–400 cm, with a narrow beam of about 15°.
                    • Soft and angled surfaces can fool it.
                    9. Microcontroller (Arduino Uno)
                    • A microcontroller reads inputs, runs code and controls outputs.
                    • Uno R3: 14 digital pins, 6 analog inputs, 5 V, 16 MHz.
                    • Keep pin current under 20 mA on an R3, and 8 mA on an R4.
                    • Pins carry signals. Power hungry parts from a separate supply.
